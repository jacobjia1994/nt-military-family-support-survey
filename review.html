A shared guide to what the questionnaire asks, why it asks it, and how the answers can help shape support.

The questionnaire follows one idea: understand everyday life, recognise support that works, identify unmet needs, then explore one current priority in more detail. The results page uses fictional records to show how analysis would work.

How the paths work

Eligibility and family relationships

The core connection is current service in the NT, with the ADF or another country’s military. Veterans who are no longer serving are outside this scope. People who are unsure can continue; their eligibility remains uncertain in the data.

Adults can select multiple relationships. A serving member can also be a partner or an adult child of another member. Adult children are included whether they live independently or rely on their parents. Financial support and daily-living support are asked separately.

Family members outside the NT have a separate location path and a reference period tied to their family member’s service in the NT. Keep these records separate from local NT service experience. The team should confirm this boundary before recruitment.

Needs, adequacy and the no-extra-support path

Adults can select 15 support areas, plus “Something else”. Young people and children see shorter, age-appropriate lists. “No need”, “Not sure” and “Prefer not to answer” are exclusive choices.

Only selected areas receive an adequacy question. The choices are enough support, some but not enough, none, not sure, and prefer not to answer. A blank answer stays blank.

Everyone can say that no additional support is needed now. That choice skips impact, help-seeking, barriers and delivery questions. A need met in the past is not treated as a current problem.

Help sought versus reasons for not seeking help

People who have sought help are asked about barriers they actually experienced. People who have not sought help see a separate set of reasons for that decision. Unknown help-seeking status is retained separately.

These answers concern one selected priority. They cannot be used as if each respondent had described barriers across every support area. Eligibility concerns and being told one is ineligible should remain distinct.

Reference periods and length

Adult and youth needs refer to the past six months, or time in the NT if shorter. Children refer to the past three months, or time here if shorter. Current priority is asked about now; its impact refers to the past four weeks or the time since the issue began.

The working targets are 8–10 minutes for adults, 5–7 for young people, and 3–5 for children. These are design targets, not measured findings. Time short and long paths with actual users before changing the public estimates.

Question count varies with selected needs. Two adequacy questions share a screen; detailed follow-up is limited to one current priority. Most answers are optional.

Children have their own perspective

Age pathways are 18+, 12–17 and 7–11. Younger children receive a short conversation guide rather than a written questionnaire. These groups guide language and presentation; they are not legal consent thresholds.

For this internal review, staff can walk through every path. Before inviting children, Lutheran Care and the responsible project team need to settle the appropriate consent and assent process, access to answers, assistance and response to disclosures. No approval or reporting arrangement is assumed by this website.

Reading or recording assistance should preserve the child’s own answers. A parent’s observation belongs in a separate account. Never replace a child’s answer simply because an adult disagrees.

Read results with the right denominator

  • Need selected: people selecting an area, divided by respondents in that view. Not selected is not the same as an explicit “no need”.
  • Support gap: some or no support, among definite adequacy answers for that area. Show unsure, declined and skipped answers separately.
  • Current priority: one choice per person; “no additional support now” is a meaningful answer.
  • Respondent counts: individuals, not unique families. Multiple members of one family may participate.
  • Separate versions: youth and child wording and domains differ. Do not pool their answers into adult percentages.

Frequency alone should not determine action. Consider impact, unmet need, less visible groups, what families value, and what the project or its partners can change. Self-selected responses describe those who took part; they do not establish population prevalence across the NT.

Useful internal review routes

  1. An adult partner from another country’s military, living in Katherine, selecting several needs and one priority.
  2. An independently living adult child, and then an adult child who relies on financial or care support.
  3. A person with no past support needs, or whose past needs were fully met.
  4. A person who needed help but did not seek it.
  5. A young person or child sharing their own experience with reading assistance.
  6. A family member outside the NT. Check that the wording follows their location.
  7. Return to an earlier page and change needs, priority or help-seeking. Check that irrelevant follow-up answers are removed.

Current handling of answers

Answers are held in page memory only. There is no submission endpoint, account, analytics tracker, local storage or contact form. Finishing does not transmit a response. A respondent can download their own JSON file. Refreshing or closing the page clears entered answers.

The results page reads only fictional records packaged with this site. Before real collection, connect an institution-managed collection service and replace the information about answer handling with the actual agreed arrangements.

Method references

The design draws on AIFS: Needs assessment, AIFS: Involving children in evaluation, and ABS: Questionnaire design. Wording here is project-specific and requires user testing; it is not a validated psychometric scale.
